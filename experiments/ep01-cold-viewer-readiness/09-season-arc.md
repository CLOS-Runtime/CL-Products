1. UNRESOLVED_TERRITORY

Inventory of what EP01 leaves genuinely open:

- Verified rent. P14: "Start with $2,000 in estimated rent. And that word matters: estimated. We hadn't verified it." P22 prices the downside: "If we get $1,850 instead of $2,000, negative $90 becomes about negative $240." The condition at P24 is "Possible — if verified rent supports the complete model." Unresolved and consequential.
- The concentrated bad month and the reserve. P28 gives "about $5,000 — roughly three mortgage payments and a starter amount," and calls it "Our first checkpoint," which implies later ones. P30 explicitly defers: "We'll put numbers around that in episode two."
- Financing the next house. P35 names three distinct sub-questions: "It depended on how much projected rent could count. What documentation we could produce. And how the timing of the transactions worked." None is answered. P37: "Not impossible. Not automatic."
- Tenant-ready cost. P40 withholds the number outright: "We didn't have a defensible total yet, so I'm not going to invent one here." P41 adds a timing problem that is separate from the total: "That money leaves during the move. Alongside closing costs. Alongside building the reserve. And before the first rent arrives."
- Personal capacity and the management decision. P47 budgets "professional management for year one," which is a one-year commitment with an implied year-two revisit. P49 opens a household thread: "this wasn't mine to score alone. My wife and I made that judgment together."
- The sell side. P54: "Meanwhile, selling never disappeared. Selling is the baseline. Keeping the house doesn't merely have to be possible. It has to beat selling." Not a single sell-side figure appears anywhere in the script — no proceeds, no equity, no transaction costs, no alternative use of the money.
- Taxes and insurance. P21: "And that's before any change in property taxes or insurance." Explicitly excluded from the central number.
- The value of the 3% mortgage itself. P07 and P08 assert that "a mortgage like that deserved caution before we gave it up," but the rate is never quantified as an asset against anything.
- The decision itself. P55: "we haven't decided to keep the house. And we haven't decided to sell it. The decision is open."

Is this five variations on one question? No. The five checks are structurally heterogeneous: an operating-margin question (P13), a solvency-under-shock question (P25–P29), a third-party gatekeeper question (P33–P36), a timing-of-capital question (P38–P43), and a human-capacity question (P45–P49). They fail by different mechanisms and would be answered by different evidence — a spreadsheet, a bank balance, a lender, contractor quotes, a conversation with a spouse. That is real variety.

The weakness is not the inventory's breadth but its resolution grammar. Four of the five yellows resolve by the same move: go get the real number. Verify rent (P24), fund the reserve (P32), get the lender's answer (P37), price the make-ready (P44). If the season is one episode per condition, the content differs but the episode shape does not — and that is a five-to-six episode structure, not ten.

Episode-sized territory EP01 opens that I would expect a season to pursue: (a) the sell-side baseline demanded by P54, which by EP01's own logic is the thing everything else must beat and which is entirely unbuilt; (b) rent verification and the $1,850 case (P14, P22); (c) the three financing sub-questions of P35, which could carry more than one episode; (d) scoping and funding the tenant-ready work plus its collision with closing costs and the reserve (P39–P41); (e) taxes and insurance on conversion, flagged and set aside at P21; (f) the year-two self-management question implied by P47; (g) revision of the five checks and the Rental Stress Test itself (P58) once reality contradicts them.

Judgment: enough raw territory, unevenly distributed. Roughly six to eight episodes are visible from EP01. The gap to ten closes only if the season goes somewhere EP01 does not point — most plausibly the sell-side, or the aftermath of an actual decision.

2. ACCIDENTAL_ANSWERS

The largest premature spend is EP02's.

P26 states EP02's insight: "Vacancy doesn't actually cost $167 every month. One month, the rent just doesn't arrive. And a roof doesn't fail $150 at a time. Let's say it fails all at once." P27 delivers the conceptual distinction EP02 would exist to teach: "the monthly set-aside and the reserve are not the same thing. The set-aside is saving at roughly the pace things wear out. The reserve protects us when something happens before we've had time to save for it." P29 supplies the exact scenario: "The tenant leaves in November. The furnace dies in December. The mortgage keeps going. The rent doesn't." P31 states the lesson: "An average that looks survivable doesn't matter if one bad month can break the plan." And P28 already puts a number on the answer: "about $5,000 — roughly three mortgage payments and a starter amount toward maintenance and larger repairs."

P30 concedes that only arithmetic remains: "We'll put numbers around that in episode two." So EP02 inherits a question whose insight, scenario, lesson and candidate answer are all in EP01. It can confirm; it cannot reveal. Worse, P28's $5,000 pre-anchors EP02's headline figure — if EP02 lands on $5,000 it is redundant, and if it lands materially higher it partially retracts P28.

Second, P13–P22 spend the whole of Check One. Every line is priced: management $350 (P16), vacancy $167 (P17), maintenance $125 (P18), capital expenses $150 (P19), arriving at "about negative $90" (P20), plus the sensitivity to "about negative $240" (P22). Check One as an analytical structure is finished; only the rent verification survives (P24). This is a defensible spend — P23's "monthly cash flow wasn't the reason to keep it at all" is EP01's thesis and requires the full stack — but it still costs the season an episode.

Third, P36 spends the season's stakes hierarchy: "If financing the next house doesn't work, the rental idea is dead. Even perfect rental economics can't rescue it." Having ranked one check above all others, EP01 tells the viewer which future episode actually matters and puts an asterisk on every other one.

Fourth, P57 spends the method in a single line: "That's the method. Name the ways the attractive number can fail. Then make it survive them." There is no methodological reveal left for the season.

Fifth, P58 spends the artifact: "We turned these same five checks into a free Rental Stress Test. It's linked in the description." The tool that could have been built across the season, or paid off at EP10, ships in the first episode.

Worth crediting as genuinely unspent: P40 is exemplary — "We didn't have a defensible total yet, so I'm not going to invent one here" withholds a number and justifies the withholding in-narrative rather than teasing it. P54 leaves the sell-side entirely unnumbered. P35 names three financing sub-questions and answers none. Those three are clean handoffs.

3. EP02_SETUP

It emerges naturally, and the seeding is load-bearing.

The EP02 question first appears at P25, not at the outro: "But there is a hidden word behind cash flow: Average. Averages smooth things out." That sentence is doing work for EP01 itself — it is the hinge that converts the Check One result (the negative $90 average at P20) into a different category of question, and without it Check Two does not exist and the five-check ladder loses a rung. The concrete mechanisms at P26 are literally de-averaging EP01's own inputs: the $167 from P17 and the $150 from P19. That is the strongest possible form of seeding — the next episode's question is generated by this episode's arithmetic rather than announced alongside it.

From there: P29 names the scenario, P30 forwards it explicitly ("We'll put numbers around that in episode two"), P31 extracts the belief change, and P60 reprises the scenario verbatim — "The tenant leaves in November. The furnace dies in December" — and poses the handoff as a quantity: "How much money does the household actually need to survive that month?" The verbatim repetition between P29 and P60 reads as deliberate callback, not filler.

So: not bolted on. The opposite problem applies. The setup is load-bearing for EP01 and over-served for EP02 — see section 2. The material that should have been EP02's opening five minutes is EP01's best passage.

One further note: P60's framing is narrower than P30's. P30 promises "numbers around that"; P60 promises a single number — "How much money does the household actually need." If EP02 delivers only that number, it is a short idea stretched, unless EP02 widens to reserve sizing policy, where the money comes from, and what holding it costs. EP01 gives no signal that the widening is coming.

4. METHOD_VS_ANECDOTE

EP01 promises both, deliberately, and the two do not collide.

Evidence for a method: P12 names the procedure — "we gave the idea five ways to fail." P13, P25, P33, P38 and P45 use a consistent "Check [N]: [Name]" template. P24, P32, P37, P44 and P50 produce a consistent scored output with a stated condition attached. P52 generalizes the output type: "Not a verdict. Conditions." P53 restates all five as a portable checklist. P56 states the method's shape as a transformation: "We started with $700 of apparent room. We ended with five conditions that have to survive contact with reality." P57 states it outright: "That's the method. Name the ways the attractive number can fail. Then make it survive them." P58 instantiates it as a transferable object.

Evidence for anecdote, or rather for scoped claims: P05, "We're documenting one real housing decision." P09, "This was our situation. Our file. Not advice for anyone else." P20, "on our own planning assumptions." P23, "On our assumptions." P36, "Nothing about that is a universal underwriting rule." P49, "My wife and I made that judgment together." P58, "It won't make the decision for you. Ours didn't make the decision for us either."

The stance is coherent: the procedure is offered as generalizable; the inputs and the verdict are not. That is honest and it is the right stance for a governed season, because it lets each episode be a particular investigation while remaining an instance of a stated method.

Is the method repeatable enough to structure ten episodes? Partly. It has two components. Enumeration — "name the ways the attractive number can fail" — is already complete and closed at P53; the season cannot re-run it, only work through it. Survival — "then make it survive them" — is the scalable half, since each condition demands its own evidence type. But P57's phrasing is thin as an operating instruction: "make it survive them" does not specify what an episode does, whereas the check-and-score grammar of P24/P32/P37/P44/P50 does. The result is a method that reliably structures about five to six episodes.

The one mechanism that could extend it to ten is visible but unstated: P51's "No green. No red" implies checks can move colors, which implies re-scoring passes. P55 gestures at the idea that the decision itself evolves — "we're no longer looking at the same decision we started with" — but no passage says re-scoring is the season's shape.

5. SUBSCRIPTION_CLARITY

Format: clear. P51 ("Five checks. Five yellows. No green. No red.") plus the Check headers establish a segmented, scored analysis. P60 previews the next episode by number and by question. A viewer knows the episodes are numbered, sequential, and attached to one continuing decision.

Subject: stated outright at P05 — "This is Calculated Landlord. We're documenting one real housing decision: whether to sell our house, or keep it and rent it out."

Register: unusually clear, and the clearest thing about the show. Restraint is performed repeatedly and specifically: P09 ("Not advice for anyone else"), P36 ("Nothing about that is a universal underwriting rule"), P40 (refusing to invent a total), P48 ("Being honest about our capacity made the economics worse. The truer plan had the worse number. We chose the truer plan anyway"), P59 ("That's what not deciding looks like"). A viewer could not mistake this for a portfolio-growth channel.

Could EP02 credibly be a different show? No. P60 names the exact scenario and the exact question, and the scenario is already familiar from P29. The risk runs the other way: EP02 is specified so completely that the viewer has little left to be curious about.

Where clarity is incomplete: the viewer learns the kind of episode but not the shape of the season. No passage says how many episodes there are, that a decision will actually be reached, or roughly when. P55 says "The decision is open" and P57 says "That's the method," but nothing commits to an ending. A subscriber cannot tell whether they are joining a finite story with a resolution or an open-ended process series. For a governed ten-episode season with EP10 closing it, that is a real omission — one sentence promising a resolution would convert episode viewers into season viewers. Note how firmly P60 sells EP02 and how nothing at all sells the season.

Second gap: the implied model of the show, from P53's five conditions, is "five more of these." That under-promises against ten and sets up a structural surprise around EP06.

6. SEASON_FUNCTION_RISKS

Risk 1 — The agenda is closed and sized for five, not ten. P12 ("five ways to fail") and P53 fix the list. A viewer holding it will expect exhaustion by roughly EP05–EP06; anything beyond reads as extension rather than plan. The remedies exist inside EP01 — re-scoring implied by P51, the sell-comparison at P54 — but neither is announced as future material.

Risk 2 — Yellow monotony. The scoring instrument produced zero discrimination on its first use: five checks, five yellows (P51). In EP01 that uniformity is a feature, and P59 justifies it: "That's what not deciding looks like." By EP05 it becomes perpetual deferral, and the color vocabulary becomes decorative. The season needs an early red or green to prove the instrument moves; EP01 gives no indication that it can beyond the bare existence of the words.

Risk 3 — Check Three's dominance (P36): "If financing the next house doesn't work, the rental idea is dead. Even perfect rental economics can't rescue it." That makes the financing episode a potential season-ender wherever it lands. Resolve it early and the remaining episodes lose their conditional charge; defer it late and every intervening episode is knowingly provisional. EP01 creates this and does not manage it.

Risk 4 — The sell side is asserted, never built. P54 sets the bar — "It has to beat selling" — with no proceeds, equity, transaction costs, or alternative use of capital anywhere in the script. EP10 must deliver a verdict that requires exactly this comparison. Built late, EP10 becomes a data dump; never built, EP10's verdict is unearned by EP01's own standard. This is the single largest structural liability EP01 creates.

Risk 5 — Reality can outrun the format. The decision is live (P55) and the show is coupled to it (P05). If the lender says no (P36) or the house sells at EP04, the remaining episodes have no subject; if nothing moves, the season is ten episodes of unresolved conditions. EP01's tightest identity move is also its biggest scheduling exposure, and no passage provides for either failure.

Risk 6 — The instrument shipped in EP01 (P58). It cannot be an EP10 payoff. And revision looks likely — P28's "first checkpoint" and P47's "year one" both imply the model changes — so the released tool will date. Recoverable, even usable as a revised-v2 beat, but EP01 reserves no right to revise it.

Risk 7 — EP02 inherits a pre-answered question (P26, P27, P29, P31, and the $5,000 at P28). The episode that determines whether viewers return has the least new information of any episode EP01 sets up.

Risk 8 — An acknowledged hole in the anchor number. P21: "And that's before any change in property taxes or insurance." The negative $90 at P20 is the figure the whole season hangs on, and EP01 tells the audience it is incomplete. Return to it and the number worsens, which is dramatically useful but softens EP01 retroactively; never return and the season's anchor stays knowingly wrong on the record. Either way it is a check EP01 wrote that the season must cash, and nothing schedules it.

7. VERDICT

EP01 functions as a season opener, with two identifiable costs. It does the essential things: it establishes a live question with real stakes (P05, P55), names a repeatable procedure (P12, P57), installs a scoring vocabulary capable of registering change (P51), commits to a distinctive register of restraint that makes the show identifiable (P09, P36, P40, P48), and hands off to EP02 out of its own arithmetic rather than by announcement (P25 to P29 to P30 to P60). Most importantly it ends genuinely undecided (P55, "The decision is open"), which is the one non-negotiable requirement of an opener and which most first episodes quietly violate. The costs: first, it overspends EP02 — P26, P27, P29 and P31 deliver EP02's mechanism, scenario and lesson, and P28's $5,000 pre-empts its likely headline number, leaving EP02 to confirm rather than reveal. Second, it publishes a closed five-item agenda (P53) sized for roughly five episodes while never signposting the two structures that would carry ten: re-scoring the yellows toward green or red, and building the sell-side baseline it asserts at P54 but never numbers. None of this is a judgment about pacing, retention or standalone quality; a cold viewer would likely experience P26–P31 as the script's strongest stretch and P54–P57 as a clean, satisfying close. It is specifically as a season opener that the first stretch is too generous and the second is too thinly built.
